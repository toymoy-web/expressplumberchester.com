How Housing Type Affects Plumbing in Chester, PA: Row, Twin, or Single-Family

Chester has a genuine mix of housing types, and each comes with real, distinct plumbing considerations worth understanding.

By the Chester Express Plumbing team · Serving Chester, PA

Chester's Real Housing Mix

Row houses and other attached homes make up the single most common housing type in Chester, making up a large share of the city's housing, a real reflection of the dense, fast construction that occurred during the city's early-1900s industrial boom.

Beyond rowhomes, Chester's housing also includes duplexes and twin homes, apartment buildings and converted multi-unit properties, and a smaller share of detached single-family homes, each with genuinely different plumbing considerations tied to how they were built and how they connect to shared infrastructure.

Chester Housing by the Census

The Census Bureau's American Community Survey 2020-2024 estimates count 14,929 housing units in the City of Chester, with a margin of error of 527. It puts the owner-occupied rate at 40.0 percent, so the other 60.0 percent of occupied homes are renter-occupied, and the median value of owner-occupied homes at $83,100.

With that many rentals, who fixes a plumbing problem often depends on a lease, so read yours and report problems in writing.

Attached homes add a second wrinkle, because neighbors can be affected by the same line. If a backup or leak shows up in one unit, mention it to the neighbor and the landlord early, so whoever is responsible for that section of pipe can be identified before the damage spreads to the next house.

Source types: U.S. Census Bureau American Community Survey 2020-2024 5-year estimates and QuickFacts.

Row Houses: Shared Walls, Sometimes Shared Lines

A row house shares structural walls with neighboring units and, in some older construction, may share or sit very close to a common sewer lateral section with an adjoining property. This shared-infrastructure pattern is part of why a sewer backup in a rowhome sometimes affects more than one household at once, and why a camera inspection is genuinely useful for tracing exactly where a shared section begins and ends.

Rowhomes also tend to have tighter basement and crawlspace access than detached construction, which affects both the cost and the approach to any leak detection, repiping, or sewer line work, since access itself is a real factor in scope and pricing.

Twin Homes: Independent Systems, Shared Structure

A twin home, two units sharing a single central wall but each with independent plumbing systems, generally does not share sewer or supply lines the way some rowhomes do, since each half was typically built with its own dedicated connections to the street. The shared wall itself is mostly a structural, not a plumbing, consideration, though noise or vibration from one side's plumbing work is sometimes noticeable to the other.

Single-Family Homes: More Independence, Different Trade-offs

Dedicated Systems

A detached single-family home has its own independent water and sewer connections with no shared-infrastructure complications.

More Yard Access

Water line and sewer line repairs often have more room to work with than a rowhome's tight urban lot.

Often Newer Construction

Chester's detached single-family stock skews somewhat newer on average than its dense rowhome core, though plenty of exceptions exist.

What This Means for You, Practically

Knowing your home's type helps set realistic expectations before a plumber even arrives. A rowhome owner dealing with a sewer backup should reasonably expect questions about neighboring properties and shared-line history as part of the diagnosis. A single-family homeowner planning a repipe can generally expect somewhat more straightforward access than a rowhome's tighter basement and party-wall constraints would allow.

None of this changes the fundamental repair process, but it does change what a realistic timeline and scope conversation looks like from the very first visit.

Multi-Unit and Converted Properties: A Fourth Category Worth Mentioning

Beyond rowhomes, twins, and single-family homes, Chester also has a real share of larger apartment buildings and properties originally built as single-family or twin homes that were later converted into multiple rental units.

These converted properties sometimes carry plumbing systems that were adequate for the original single-family use but are now supporting considerably more fixtures and daily usage than they were designed for, a real, checkable reason water heater capacity and drain line adequacy are worth specifically evaluating in any converted multi-unit property rather than assumed to be fine simply because the building has been standing for decades.

Why This Matters When Buying or Renting in Chester

Understanding a property's housing type before signing a lease or a purchase agreement gives you a realistic starting point for what plumbing questions are actually worth asking. According to standard home inspection practice, a prospective rowhome buyer should ask directly about sewer lateral history and any known shared-line arrangements with neighbors.

A buyer looking at a converted multi-unit property should ask whether the plumbing was actually upgraded during conversion or simply subdivided from the original single-family system.

These are specific, answerable questions that a seller, agent, or property manager should be able to address directly. Asking them upfront is considerably easier than discovering the answer after move-in, when the same information costs a service call to uncover.

Ask during the search, before any lease or purchase agreement has been signed, when the answer still genuinely affects your decision. A seller or landlord unwilling to answer directly is itself useful information about what you might be taking on, since the plumbing behind the walls is one of the more expensive things to discover after the fact.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do rowhomes really share sewer lines with neighbors?

Some of Chester's older rowhomes, built in dense early-1900s construction, do have shared or closely adjacent lateral sections, though this varies by specific property and construction era. A camera inspection can confirm the actual layout for a specific address.

Is plumbing work more expensive in a rowhome than a single-family home?

It can be, mainly due to tighter access in basements and crawlspaces and, in some cases, the added complexity of confirming shared versus independent lines. Cost still depends on the specific job, not housing type alone.

Do twin homes share plumbing between the two units?

Generally no. Twin homes typically have independent water and sewer connections for each unit despite sharing a central structural wall, unlike some older rowhome construction with shared laterals.

Is Chester's housing mostly rowhomes, or is there more variety?

Row houses and other attached homes are the single most common type, but the city also has a real mix of twins, duplexes, apartment buildings, and detached single-family homes, each with its own plumbing considerations.

Does owning or renting change who fixes plumbing in Chester?

Your lease usually spells out who handles repairs in a rental. The Census Bureau puts Chester's owner-occupied rate at 40.0 percent for 2020-2024, so many households rent. Report problems to your landlord in writing.
