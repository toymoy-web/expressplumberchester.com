Chester's Nearly $100 Million Revitalization: What It Means for Older Homes

A real, current state investment in Chester's infrastructure and housing stock. Here's what's actually in it and what it could mean for a homeowner's plumbing.

By the Chester Express Plumbing team · Serving Chester, PA

A Real, Current Investment in Chester

In 2026, Governor Josh Shapiro announced a nearly $100 million investment to revitalize Chester, combining state and private funding aimed at road and pedestrian infrastructure, blight remediation, and economic development support. According to the Governor's office, the Commonwealth's portion alone was reported at roughly $75.5 million, intended to help spur more than $97 million in total investment across the city.

This follows years of prior effort, including the Chester Waterfront Master Plan developed by the Riverfront Alliance of Delaware County starting in 2019, aimed at redeveloping roughly 100 acres of riverfront between Highland Avenue and Norris Street. Chester has been under Pennsylvania's Act 47 financial recovery oversight since 1995, and both of these efforts are part of a longer push to change that trajectory.

What the State Announced

The Governor's office announced on September 10, 2026 a revitalization effort totaling $97,100,863, which it rounds to nearly $100 million, with $75.5 million from the Commonwealth and the rest from business leaders, nonprofits, and city and county government. Listed items include $52.57 million for a Route 291 and U.S. 13 redesign, $27.5 million for a waterfront sportsplex, $7.25 million for Edgemont Avenue pedestrian improvements and $2.6 million for blight demolition and site readiness.

The announcement also lists home repairs for owner-occupied houses. We do not have details on eligibility or how to apply, so read the Governor's press release and ask the City of Chester.

Source types: Pennsylvania Governor's Office press releases dated September 10, 2026.

What This Could Mean for Plumbing, Practically Speaking

Blight Remediation

Addressing vacant or deteriorating properties can mean more properties getting brought back into use, often with plumbing systems that have sat unused and need real assessment before reoccupancy.

Infrastructure Investment

Road and pedestrian infrastructure work sometimes intersects with underground utility work, including water and sewer lines, though the scope of any specific project is worth confirming directly with the city.

Rising Property Values

Revitalization efforts that succeed tend to increase home values over time, which is often when owners decide a planned repipe or major upgrade makes more financial sense than deferred maintenance.

A Practical Note for Homeowners Right Now

None of this changes what your specific home needs today. A home with original 1910s-1920s galvanized supply lines has the same practical maintenance needs whether the city around it is being actively revitalized or not.

What a genuine, well-funded revitalization effort can mean is a better case for investing in your own property now, rather than waiting, if you're weighing whether repairs on an older home are worth it in a neighborhood that's realistically expected to see reinvestment over the coming years.

If you're buying a previously vacant or long-neglected property as part of this broader revitalization, plan on a full plumbing inspection before assuming the existing system is usable as-is. A property sitting empty for years sometimes has plumbing issues that only show up once water runs through the system again.

What to Check Before Reoccupying a Long-Vacant Property

  • Have the water service confirmed as active and pressure-tested before assuming any fixture works correctly.
  • Check for signs of a slow leak that went unnoticed while the property sat empty, water stains, warped flooring, or a musty smell.
  • Have a technician inspect the water heater before relighting or restarting it after an extended vacancy.
  • Confirm the sewer line is clear with a camera inspection, since tree roots and settling can affect an unused line just as much as one in regular use.

A Genuinely Good Long-Term Sign

Public investment on this scale, backed by state government funding and coordinated with private development, is a meaningfully different situation than a one-off announcement that quietly fades. Whether or not any specific project touches your block directly, a real, funded commitment to Chester's infrastructure is a reasonable factor to weigh if you've been putting off a decision about whether an older home here is worth investing in for the years ahead.

The Waterfront Plan Adds Another Layer

Separate from the 2026 state investment, the Chester Waterfront Master Plan has been in development since 2019 through the Riverfront Alliance of Delaware County, working with the architecture firm NBBJ on a multi-phase redevelopment of roughly 100 acres along the river between Highland Avenue and Norris Street.

The plan's stated goals include better pedestrian and cycling connectivity, activating existing assets like Barry Bridge Park and the Riverwalk trail, and addressing stormwater management as part of broader environmental sustainability planning for the waterfront.

For a homeowner in one of the neighborhoods closer to the waterfront specifically, stormwater management improvements are worth paying attention to over time, since they can affect how a property's own drainage and sump pump needs are evaluated going forward, though any specific impact on an individual property is worth confirming directly with the city as these plans move from design into construction phases.

What to Actually Do About Your Own Property Right Now

None of this changes the practical steps available to you today. If you suspect an aging supply line or a slab leak, the right move is still the same: call a technician, get an honest assessment, and get a real repair cost before committing to anything, regardless of what the city does or does not build nearby.

Scheduling a proactive inspection now, rather than waiting for an emergency, is the one action guaranteed to help your specific home, whatever timeline the broader revitalization ends up following.

A History Worth Knowing Before Reading Too Much Into Any One Announcement

Chester has been under Pennsylvania's Act 47 program for financially distressed municipalities since 1995, and previous waterfront revitalization efforts have faced real setbacks over the decades, including a prior plan that stalled during an economic downturn before it could be fully carried out.

None of that is a reason for cynicism about the current effort specifically, but it is a reason to treat any single announcement as one step in a long process rather than an overnight transformation, and to make property decisions based on your own home's actual needs rather than speculation about how quickly broader change will arrive.

The most useful takeaway for a homeowner is fairly simple: real public and private money is genuinely flowing into Chester right now, which is a meaningfully different situation than in years past, and that is worth factoring into a long-term view of the city, alongside, not instead of, taking care of what your own property actually needs today.

A well-maintained plumbing system is one of the few things a homeowner can control directly regardless of how quickly the surrounding neighborhood changes, and it is usually the more immediately practical place to focus attention and budget, whatever pace the broader revitalization ends up taking in your specific part of the city, and whatever the final scope of any individual infrastructure project turns out to be once it moves from announcement to construction.
