Water Taste, Smell, or Look Off?

Chester's municipal water, supplied by Chester Water Authority from a surface-water source, is treated to meet safe drinking water standards, but treated and great-tasting aren't always the same thing. A chlorine taste or smell right after treatment, occasional mineral taste, or a preference for better-tasting water without buying bottled water are the most common reasons Chester homeowners look into filtration, not usually a specific contamination concern. This same vintage shows up across Chester, from the Sun Village neighborhood to the streets near Widener University.

Filtration and softening solve different problems: filtration targets taste, odor, chlorine, and sediment, while a softener addresses mineral hardness specifically. A technician can help sort out which one, or both, actually fits what you're noticing.

2

Filtration Options

Whole-Home Filtration

Installed at the main water line, this treats every tap in the house, including showers, for chlorine taste and sediment.

Under-Sink Filtration

A more affordable option focused on drinking and cooking water at one or two specific taps.

Reverse Osmosis

The most thorough filtration available for a single tap, typically installed under the kitchen sink for drinking water specifically.

What Installation Looks Like

A whole-home system is installed at the point where the main water line enters the house, typically completed in a few hours with a brief water shutoff. An under-sink or reverse osmosis system installs directly at the cabinet under the sink and doesn't affect water anywhere else in the house.

Ongoing filter replacement is part of any system and gets discussed at installation so there are no surprises about maintenance intervals or costs down the line.

Matching the System to the Actual Problem

Not every complaint needs a whole-home system. A chlorine taste noticed only at the kitchen tap is often better and more affordably solved with an under-sink unit than a whole-house install, while sediment or odor affecting every fixture, including showers, points toward a point-of-entry system instead. Confirming which pattern you're actually seeing before recommending equipment avoids selling more system than the problem calls for.

Reverse osmosis is worth a specific mention for anyone using a lot of bottled water for drinking or cooking, since it typically produces noticeably cleaner-tasting water than a standard carbon filter alone. A dedicated faucet is usually installed alongside a reverse osmosis system so filtered water stays separate from the regular tap, and it typically fits into the same sink cutout pattern most kitchens already have room for.

What to Check Before You Choose a System

  • A noticeable chlorine taste or smell, especially right after the tap is turned on.
  • Occasional cloudiness that clears after the water sits for a minute.
  • A preference for better-tasting water without relying on bottled water.
  • Visible sediment in a faucet aerator or showerhead over time.
  • Wanting cleaner water specifically for cooking or drinking without treating the whole house.

What Water Filtration Costs

Cost depends on system type and household size. Filter cartridges for either system are widely available and easy to source locally, so ongoing maintenance doesn't depend on a specialty supplier, and most standard cartridges can be swapped by a homeowner between scheduled service visits. Sediment filters in particular need more frequent changes in older homes with galvanized supply lines, since corrosion byproducts add to the particulate load faster than in a newer copper or PEX system, which is worth mentioning when choosing a filter's rated capacity and change interval so it isn't sized for average conditions that don't quite match your home's actual plumbing. Frequently Asked Questions

Is Chester's tap water safe to drink without a filter?

Chester Water Authority treats municipal water to meet safe drinking water standards. A filter isn't a safety requirement for most homes; it's typically about taste, odor, or personal preference rather than a health necessity.

What's the difference between filtration and a water softener?

Filtration targets taste, odor, chlorine, and sediment. A water softener specifically addresses mineral hardness. They solve different problems and some homes benefit from both.

Does a whole-home filter affect water pressure?

A properly sized system shouldn't cause a noticeable pressure drop. Sizing the system to household water usage is part of a correct installation.

How often do filters need to be replaced?

It varies by system and household water usage, typically every 3 to 12 months depending on the filter type. This gets discussed at installation so the schedule is clear from the start.
